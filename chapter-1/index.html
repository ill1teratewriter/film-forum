The two roads

Straight on, or a left at the Vista

Hollywood Boulevard runs east until it hits Sunset, and there is a theatre standing on the corner where they meet. Every chapter the room decides at that corner, and this one went straight on.

Hollywood Blvd. Voted Field guide Casablanca 1942 · 102 min · dir. Michael Curtiz · Warner Bros. Won 3 Academy Awards in 1944, including Best Picture

The blueprint, run at full power. You can see every joint and feel every turn coming, and it still works better here than it has worked anywhere since. That is not a flaw in the film, that is the entire case for the blueprint.

Hollywood Blvd. · Field Guide

Casablanca

1942 · Warner Bros. · dir. Michael Curtiz · 102 min · b/w
01

Before you press play

Optional trivia.
Half the script did not exist when the cameras rolled

Shooting ran May 25 to August 3, 1942, and unusually it was shot mostly in sequence, because only the first half of the screenplay was ready. New pages arrived on set each morning. Howard Koch, flatly: "When we began, we didn't have a finished script." Curtiz told Ingrid Bergman to play it "in between."

One correction to the legend, since you'll hear it repeated: it is not true that nobody knew how the film ended. The writers had that argued out in memos before a frame was shot, and the Production Code left them very little room. What was genuinely unresolved was the film's last line, which Bogart came back to dub a month after the wrap.

The people in the background are not acting like refugees

Almost the entire supporting cast was foreign born, and a great many of them, credited and not, had fled the exact thing the film is about. Historian Aljean Harmetz: they brought to a dozen small roles "an understanding and a desperation that could never have come from Central Casting." The director, Michael Curtiz, was a Hungarian Jewish émigré born Mihály Kertész.

Conrad Veidt, who plays Major Strasser, was German and not Jewish. When Goebbels made the film industry fill out a racial questionnaire in 1933, Veidt wrote "Jude" rather than renounce his Jewish wife. He left for Britain, gave a large share of his savings to the British war effort, and wrote into his Hollywood contract that any German he played must be a villain. He was the highest paid actor on the picture and died six months after it opened.

Marcel Dalio, the croupier, had been a major star in France. The Nazis used posters of his face as the image of "a typical Jew." The rest of his family died in the camps. Madeleine Lebeau, who plays Yvonne, was nineteen, and escaped Paris with Dalio on Chilean visas that turned out to be forgeries.

Nobody involved thought they were making anything special

Budgeted at $878,000, delivered at $1,039,000. Shot almost entirely on the Warner Bros. Burbank lot. The Paris street is a set that had just been built for The Desert Song and redressed.

Then history did the studio a favour it could not have bought. The Allies landed at Casablanca on November 8, 1942. Warner Bros. rushed the premiere to November 26. Roosevelt and Churchill met in Casablanca that January, and the film went into general release on January 23, 1943, inside the conference window.

A counter fact worth keeping in your pocket: the Office of War Information blocked screenings of the film for American troops stationed in North Africa, worried it would inflame Vichy sympathisers in the region.

While you watch

Spoilers inside. Open each one only when you reach it.
~10:00Rick's first appearanceOpen
The hand before the face

The camera cranes down into the café and drifts table to table, eavesdropping. Then it settles on a close up of a hand signing a check "OK Rick", and only then tilts up to Bogart, alone, playing chess against himself. He doesn't look up.

Why it matters: the film spends ten full minutes letting everyone talk about a man before it will show him to you, then introduces him signature first, face second. He is a reputation before he is a person. And a man playing chess against himself is the whole movie in one image.

~32:00Somebody asks for a songOpen
Watch Sam, not Ilsa

When the song is requested, watch the piano player instead. Sam tries to steer her off it, and keeps glancing toward the back of the room. He is watching for Rick before you are. Then the cut: Rick striding over, furious, and the sentence dies mid word.

Why it matters: the single biggest turn in the film is executed with blocking and a broken sentence rather than dialogue. The line breaks partly because the Hays Office made them cut Rick's original version of it, which had a swear word in it.

~70:00The band starts playingOpen
The faces, not the song

Curtiz cuts away from his stars into a run of tight close ups of extras: the croupier, the waiter, and Yvonne, who has spent the night drinking with a German officer and now stands up singing with tears running down her face.

Why it matters: this is where the private story gets steamrolled by the public one, and it isn't quite acting. A witness on set said many of the performers were crying and he "realized that they were all real refugees." Leslie Epstein, on Lebeau's close up: "They're not tears of glycerin shed by an actress."

03

The shape of it

The three act structure, mapped onto the actual film

A long flat setup, a climb that never quite stops climbing, one peak, and a landing that takes about five minutes. That is the whole three act structure in one line. Each mark is a moment where the film turns and does not turn back. The first two are named so you can see how to read it. The rest are yours to find, and they're the conversation on Sunday. Timings are approximate, so give them a couple of minutes either way.

04

Open after the credits

"Play it again, Sam" is never said in this film

Ilsa says "Play it once, Sam. For old times' sake… Play it, Sam. Play 'As Time Goes By.'" Rick says "You played it for her, you can play it for me… Play it!" The famous line comes from decades of people misremembering, and later from Woody Allen putting it on a marquee.

Dooley Wilson could not play the piano

He was a professional drummer, borrowed from Paramount. Watch his hands on a rewatch. Who actually dubbed it is still genuinely disputed: credited for decades to Elliot Carpenter, but payroll records and a forensic musicologist point instead to studio pianist Jean Plummer. Warner Bros. also considered dubbing Wilson's singing voice, and thankfully didn't.

The plane in the last scene is a plywood cutout

Balsa and plywood models, worked by little person actors in mechanics' overalls to fake the scale. That is what the fog is for. Assistant director Lee Katz: "To give it forced perspective. And it worked." Real aircraft footage was shot separately at Van Nuys and cut in.

"Here's looking at you, kid" isn't in any draft of the screenplay

It reportedly came from something Bogart said to Bergman off camera while she played poker between takes with her English coach. And it wasn't his either. It is a documented American bar toast going back to the New York Herald in March 1871. It is said four times in the film.

The Ronald Reagan casting story is a myth

Warner Bros.' publicity department planted an item in the Hollywood Reporter on January 5, 1942, saying Ann Sheridan and Ronald Reagan would co-star. The screenplay hadn't been started. No casting had happened. Reagan was an Army Reserve lieutenant awaiting call up and couldn't have taken a spring start. Hal Wallis wanted Bogart from the beginning, and a studio memo records the picture was "being written for him."

The half true part: Ann Sheridan genuinely was considered for Ilsa, before Wallis traded Olivia de Havilland's services to Selznick to get Bergman.

A haircut saved "As Time Goes By"

Composer Max Steiner disliked the song and lobbied to replace it with an original. Doing so would have meant reshooting every scene built around it, which was impossible, because Bergman had already cut her hair short for For Whom the Bell Tolls.

The word "Jew" never appears in the film

The Bulgarian couple are fleeing "persecution," unspecified. Warner Bros. was the most committed anti-Nazi studio in Hollywood and still avoided naming the Jewish identity of its own staff and stars. Worth sitting with.

Sunset Blvd. · Field Guide

Sullivan's Travels

1941 · Paramount · written & directed by Preston Sturges · 90 min · b/w
01

Before you press play

Optional trivia.
This movie is picking a fight with other movies

Sturges, in his own memoir: "After I saw a couple of pictures put out by some of my fellow comedy-directors, which seemed to have abandoned the fun in favor of the message, I wrote Sullivan's Travels to satisfy an urge to tell them that they were getting a little too deep-dish; to leave the preaching to the preachers."

A studio executive in the film says the words "What's the matter with Capra?" out loud.

Worth being precise about: Sturges named Capra in the script, but never said publicly that Capra was who he meant. Everyone assumes it.

The man who invented the job of writer-director

In August 1939, Sturges handed Paramount a screenplay he had been sitting on for six years, The Great McGinty, for ten dollars, on the single condition that they let him direct it. They gave him $350,000, a three week schedule and cheap talent. He won the first Academy Award for Best Original Screenplay ever given.

The credit read "Written and Directed by Preston Sturges," and by most accounts it was the first time those two verbs had appeared together on a talkie.

The ten dollars was partly studio publicity, since he was a contract writer on roughly $2,500 a week. But the trade was real, and it is why the directing chair stopped belonging exclusively to directors.

It cost $689,665.16, and the leading lady was six months pregnant

Shot May 12 to July 22, 1941, and came in $86,665.16 over budget. Veronica Lake was six months pregnant when principal photography began. Edith Head designed the costumes to conceal it and a former Rose Bowl queen doubled her for the physical work. Her daughter was born a month after the wrap.

Accounts differ sharply on when Sturges found out. Lake's version: "It took physical restraint to keep him from boiling over at me." Other accounts say only Edith Head and Sturges's wife knew.

02

While you watch

Spoilers inside. Open each one only when you reach it.
0:00The very first shotOpen
It opens on someone else's ending

Two men fighting on a speeding train, then "The End." You are watching the last two minutes of a movie that does not exist. The camera pulls back into a screening room, and the argument that follows is reportedly ten script pages in one unbroken take, after the cinematographer dared Sturges to shoot it without a cut.

Why it matters: the thesis of the whole film is in the first five minutes, staged so you can't mistake it for a scene.

~45:00When everyone goes quietOpen
The talking stops

Sturges is the most word drunk filmmaker of his era. Then, for about seven minutes, everyone simply stops talking and music carries the picture.

Why it matters: he takes away his own best weapon. The silence is how the film tells you Sullivan has reached somewhere his script cannot cover. It took seven hours to shoot, four hours over schedule, the biggest overrun on the picture.

~1:12A sheet drops from the raftersOpen
Watch the framing, not the cartoon

For eighty minutes Sullivan has been the subject of every shot. Here he becomes one face in a crowd.

Why it matters: the film makes its whole point with composition, several minutes before anybody says it out loud.

03

The shape of it

The same diagram, on a film with four genres in it

Same diagram, different problem. Casablanca's curve is tension. This one is tone, and the film falls off a cliff with it around the forty five minute mark. Four genres in ninety minutes, and Sturges does not smooth a single seam. The first two marks are named so you can see how to read it. The rest are yours to find.

04

Open after the credits

The Coen brothers stole the title of the movie Sullivan wants to make

The grim Depression epic Sullivan is desperate to direct is called O Brother, Where Art Thou? It doesn't exist. In 2000 the Coens made a film with that title, which is, fittingly, a comedy.

Sturges wanted Chaplin for the church scene and was turned down

The cartoon the chain gang watches is Disney's "Playful Pluto" (1934), famous among animators for the flypaper gag, the first time an animated character really seemed to be thinking. Sturges asked to use Charlie Chaplin footage instead and was refused, which is interesting given that McCrea does a Little Tramp impression earlier in the film.

The head of the NAACP wrote to thank Sturges, and the actor went uncredited

NAACP secretary Walter White wrote to Sturges: "I want to congratulate and thank you for the church sequence in Sullivan's Travels. This is one of the most moving scenes I have seen in a moving picture for a long time."

And the preacher who delivers that welcome, Jess Lee Brooks, was left off the credits. The same film also contains a genuinely ugly racial gag in the land yacht chase. Both things are true at once, and that is worth an argument.

The U.S. government banned it from wartime export

The Office of Censorship formally disapproved the film for export during the war, specifically citing "the long sequence showing life in a prison chain gang" as unsuitable material to send abroad.

1941 critics could not agree what they had just watched

The New York Times: "the most brilliant picture yet this year," and put it on their ten best list. The New Yorker: "anyone can make a mistake, Preston Sturges, even. The mistake in question is a pretentious number called Sullivan's Travels." The Hollywood Reporter: "Laughter is the thing people want, not social studies."

Sturges himself noted that one reviewer wanted to know "what the hell the tragic passages were doing in this comedy," and another wanted to know "what the comic passages were doing in a drama." It entered the National Film Registry in 1990.

Sturges didn't know his own ending when he started writing

"When I started writing it I had no idea what Sullivan was going to discover… When I got down to there I found he still had one thing left: the ability to laugh."

The closing line, in full, because most people quote it wrong

"There's a lot to be said for making people laugh. Did you know that's all some people have? It isn't much, but it's better than nothing in this cockeyed caravan."

The version that gets passed around drops "Did you know that's all some people have?", which is the only part of the sentence with any teeth in it.

Why we start here

One is the perfect engine of classic Hollywood storytelling. The other was built, on purpose, to take that engine apart while it's still running.

In 1941, Preston Sturges made a movie about a director who has decided that the movies he is good at are worthless. One year later, Warner Bros. made a formula picture on a modest budget, on borrowed sets, with a script that wasn't finished when shooting started, and accidentally filmed the best argument anyone has ever made for exactly the kind of movie Sturges was defending. Neither one knew about the other. They have been arguing ever since.

Watch one. Or both. Then bring eighty four years of movies with you, and tell me what these two started.

The study

Classical Hollywood foundations

What “structure” means, and why we start in the 1940s.

Why we start with structure

We're not just randomly watching films. We're following an overall story that will help us understand the medium better, and broaden what we even think is possible.

Chapter one is all about structure: how a story is presented, and how almost everything still follows, or deliberately rebels against, an ancient format that has been reintroduced and split apart for thousands of years.

If you've ever watched a movie and thought "how did they do that?", if you've ever loved a film so much you wanted the poster, the quote on a mug, the whole world of it, this is for you.

The shape everyone draws

A film is a contained experience, usually 90 to 120 minutes. That length matches how we hold attention in a dark room. Something happens, obstacles rise, stakes escalate, the story resolves. Cause leads to effect. Structure is the invisible skeleton we rarely notice when it's working perfectly.

Here is the diagram you have seen a hundred times.

Top row: the three acts. Bottom row: the four parts Kristin Thompson found when she actually measured Hollywood films. Act II is not one thing.

It is worth knowing where that shape came from, because almost none of it came from film.

Freytag was writing about tragedy, and he counted five

Gustav Freytag published Die Technik des Dramas in 1863. He was analysing Sophocles, Shakespeare, and the German five act play. His model did not have three parts, rather, five.

  1. Einleitung, the introduction
  2. Steigerung, the rise
  3. Höhepunkt, the climax
  4. Fall oder Umkehr, the fall or the turn
  5. Katastrophe

He was theorizing a structure that leads to catastrophe, rather than the school taught resolution stories we see all the time now. That last beat is the one that got quietly swapped out somewhere along the way.

Freytag died in 1895, the same year cinema went public. That December the Lumière brothers ran ten short films in the basement of a Paris café and sold tickets, the screening history decided to remember, even though Skladanowsky in Berlin and Latham in New York had both got there first.

Aristotle is the other main voice people reach for, usually by way of the Poetics. His beginning, middle, and end was a definition of wholeness, and was never meant as a blueprint. When he actually divides a tragedy, in chapter 18, he uses two halves, desis and lusis, the binding and the unbinding. The Greek plays he was writing about had no act structure at all.

The rulebook came 37 years after Casablanca

Funny enough, the three act rulebook was published in 1979, in Syd Field's Screenplay. He attached page numbers to it.

  1. Act I, pages 1 to 30
  2. Act II, pages 31 to 90
  3. Act III, pages 91 to 120
  4. Plot points landing around page 25 and page 85

Casablanca and Citizen Kane, the two films everyone reaches for when they want to demonstrate the format, were made in 1942 and 1941. Neither was ever taught it the way we have been, because it did not exist yet.

Field was describing what films already did, and development executives then adopted it as a filter. David Bordwell, on the mechanism:

Once the three-act template became public knowledge, development executives embraced it as a way to make script acquisition routine.

A description hardened into a rule because it made saying no easier. And when Bordwell went into the archives looking for studio era writers who actually worked in acts, he came back with this:

I couldn't find any script draft broken into acts.

He found one exception. F. Scott Fitzgerald, writing Infidelity for MGM in 1938, laid out eight numbered sequences and grouped them into a first act of 45 pages, a second of 50, and a third of 25, nothing like the 30/60/30 that would come later. His working unit was the sequence, not the act.

So what were they actually doing

Kristin Thompson counted. In Storytelling in the New Hollywood, from 1999, she argues mainstream films break into four large parts rather than three acts, setup, complicating action, development, and climax, with a short epilogue after. Each one runs roughly 25 to 35 minutes.

Long films are the tell, they do not get longer acts, they get more parts. A three hour film tends to have six.

That is the bottom row on the diagram. The famously protracted second act is not one long act at all, it is two parts with a major turn sitting between them, which is why the midpoint of a film feels like a hinge rather than a middle.

What actually shaped a 1940s picture

Runtime was set by exhibition. In 1938 the average Warner Bros. feature ran 75 minutes, MGM's ran 88. Double bills meant two features plus a newsreel, a cartoon, and a short all had to fit a turnover schedule, so the ceiling on length was set by the theatre's clock.

The Production Code taxed the third act specifically. It permitted a great deal in acts one and two, provided the ending restored the moral ledger. That is why so many pictures from this period have final reels that feel bolted on.

And the thing that did not shape it, the reel. A 1,000 foot reel runs about eleven minutes, and you will hear that reel changes created act breaks. There is no evidence for it anywhere in the scholarship. Changeover projection exists precisely to make the reel change invisible to the audience, and eleven does not divide into thirty.

Why we're starting here

This era crystallized the rules under real pressure. Budgets were tight. Locations were limited. Stories had to be efficient and emotionally clear. The war made personal drama feel urgent and universal. Filmmakers couldn't rely on spectacle, so they relied on script, character, and invisible craft.

So we are not here to learn the rules. We are here to learn the shape that a lot of people later mistook for rules, because that is the only way to tell, when you sit down in front of anything, whether a film is following it or fighting it.

Both roads end here

Two films set out to do the exact opposite thing, and both of them got there. One proved the rules work by obeying them without wasting a single move. The other proved the rules are negotiable by walking away from them halfway through and arriving anyway.

Next time you're in a theatre, ask which one the film is doing.
