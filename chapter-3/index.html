Chapter 3 · Point of View. Whose eyes the story lends you, and what it costs to borrow them.

The two roads

Straight on, or a left at the Vista

Hollywood Boulevard runs east until it hits Sunset, and there is a theatre standing on the corner where they meet. Every chapter the room decides at that corner.

Two films about the act of watching, built from opposite ends of the lens. In Rear Window you are the watcher: you sit in one man’s chair, you see what he sees, and the film almost never lets you leave the room. In The Truman Show you are the audience of a man being watched by everyone, and the film keeps swapping whose eyes you are using — his, the hidden cameras’, the control room’s, the viewers’ at home.

One locks the point of view down tighter than almost any film ever made. The other splits it five ways. Both end with somebody looking straight back.

Hollywood Blvd. Rear Window Not selected 1954 · 112 min · dir. Alfred Hitchcock · Paramount

A photographer stuck at home with a broken leg starts watching his neighbours across the courtyard, and becomes sure one of them has committed murder.

Download the Rear Window paper edition (PDF)

Why it is on the ballot. It is the purest point-of-view experiment in the Hollywood canon. For almost two hours the camera stays inside one apartment and shows you what one man in a wheelchair can see. Everything you know, you know because he looked.

01

Before you press play

Optional trivia.

The whole world of the film is one set

The courtyard was built as a single composite set on Paramount’s Stage 18, roughly 185 feet long and 98 feet wide, holding 31 apartments. The stage floor was dug out so the courtyard sat below stage level and Jeff’s second-floor window looked down on it from ground level. Sources disagree on how many apartments were fully furnished: the studio’s own notes say eight, other accounts say twelve.

The neighbours could not hear the director

Hitchcock stayed in Jeff’s apartment and cued the actors across the courtyard by short-wave radio, through flesh-coloured earpieces they wore on camera.

It disappeared for a decade

The rights to five Hitchcock films, Rear Window among them, reverted to him, and he kept them out of circulation. They became known as the “Lost Hitchcocks.” Rear Window only came back in a Universal re-release in October 1983, three years after his death.

02

While you watch

Open each one only when you reach it.

First three minutes The story before a word is spoken Open

The blinds rise with the credits. The camera drifts across the courtyard, finds a thermometer in the nineties, a sleeping man, a signed plaster cast, a smashed camera, a photograph of a racing crash, a magazine cover. You know who he is, what he does and how he got hurt before anybody speaks.

Why it matters: The film teaches you its rule in the first shot. The camera shows you things, and you do the reading.

Every time Jeff looks Look, see, react Open

Watch the pattern: a close-up of Jeff looking, a shot of what he sees, then his face again. Hitchcock described that three-part structure to François Truffaut as “the purest expression of a cinematic idea.”

Why it matters: The neighbours never change. What changes is his face, and the meaning you read into it.

While Jeff is asleep The film cheats, once Open

In the small hours the camera pans away from the sleeping Jeff on its own and shows Thorwald leaving the building with a woman. Jeff never sees it.

Why it matters: It is the film’s one real break from its own rule, and it works against his theory at the exact moment you are committing to it. Ask yourself afterwards whether the film lied to him, or told the truth to you.

The look back He sees you Open

Across the courtyard, a man turns his head and looks straight down the lens.

Why it matters: For the whole film, looking has been one way. The moment the watched one looks back, the rules break, and the next ten minutes come for the room you have been sitting in.

03

After the credits

Everything here gives the film away. Keep it for afterwards.

Open The climax borrows someone else’s eyes Open

During the flashbulb scene the film cuts four times to Thorwald’s dazzled vision, rendered through a red filter. It is one of the only times the camera belongs to somebody other than Jeff, and the film saves it for the moment he is most defenceless.

Open It came from a pulp story, and the women were invented Open

The source is Cornell Woolrich’s “It Had to Be Murder,” published in Dime Detective in February 1942. There is no Lisa in it and no Stella. Screenwriter John Michael Hayes built both women out of the story’s male houseman. The film’s biggest addition is the person who actually does the dangerous part.

Open The courtyard had its own weather department Open

The set was lit with a reported thousand-plus units and fitted with a sprinkler system for the thunderstorm. Jeff’s world, like Truman’s, was a place where somebody else controlled the sun and the rain. Hitchcock’s cameo is inside it: he is the man winding the clock in the songwriter’s apartment.

Sunset Blvd. The Truman Show Not selected 1998 · 103 min · dir. Peter Weir · written by Andrew Niccol · Paramount

An insurance salesman slowly realises his whole life has been a television show, broadcast around the clock to the whole world.

Download the The Truman Show paper edition (PDF)

Why it is on the ballot. Where Rear Window never lets you out of one man’s eyes, this film cannot decide whose eyes you should be using, and that is the point. You watch Truman, you watch the people watching Truman, and you watch the man who decides what everyone sees. By the end you have to ask which audience you belong to.

01

Before you press play

Optional trivia.

It arrived before reality TV did

It opened on 5 June 1998. Dutch Big Brother began fifteen months later, in September 1999, and Survivor reached American television in May 2000. The film predicted a genre before the genre had a name.

Seahaven is a real town

The picture-perfect island was shot largely in Seaside, Florida, a planned resort town on the Gulf coast that already looked like that. The production barely had to dress it.

Christof was cast twice, and the second one had a weekend to prepare

Dennis Hopper was cast as the show’s creator and left after filming began. Ed Harris came in at the last minute: “It was a Friday. I think I started work on Monday.” He was nominated for an Oscar for it.

02

While you watch

Open each one only when you reach it.

The opening credits Whose film is starting? Open

The first credits you see are the show’s, not the movie’s. Truman Burbank is billed “as himself.”

Why it matters: Before the story begins, the film has told you that you are watching the programme, not a movie about it.

Watch the edges Hidden-camera shots Open

A great many shots are taken from odd places, with dark vignetted corners like a keyhole: a car radio, a button, a bathroom mirror. Cinematographer Peter Biziou built them with very wide lenses and masks in front of the glass, and one camera really was a lens dropped into the cavity of Truman’s car radio.

Why it matters: Those shots are the show’s point of view. Notice when the film switches to a clean, normal camera, and ask whose eyes those are.

Early on Something falls out of the sky Open

A studio light crashes into the street in front of Truman. Read the label: “Sirius (9 Canis Major).”

Why it matters: It is the first crack in the world, and the film hands you the joke before Truman understands it. Being a step ahead of him is its own kind of point of view. Sirius is really Alpha Canis Majoris, so even the label is a fake.

Mid-argument The cocoa Open

In the middle of a real fight, Meryl turns toward an unseen camera: “Why don’t you let me fix you some of this new Mococoa drink? All natural cocoa beans from the upper slopes of Mount Nicaragua. No artificial sweeteners.”

Why it matters: It is funny and horrible at once. She is performing for a camera Truman cannot see, and for a second, so are you.

03

After the credits

Everything here gives the film away. Keep it for afterwards.

Open The script started much darker Open

Andrew Niccol’s draft was a thriller set in a rain-soaked artificial New York, with a drinking, unravelling Truman. Weir moved it into bright suburban daylight, on the reasoning that nobody would watch something that grim twenty-four hours a day. The trap is harder to see because it is sunny.

Open It gave its name to a real condition Open

In 2008 the brothers Joel Gold, a psychiatrist, and Ian Gold, a philosopher of psychiatry, described patients convinced they were being filmed for a show. They called it the “Truman Show delusion.”

Open Three Oscar nominations, no wins Open

Ed Harris for Supporting Actor, Peter Weir for Directing, Andrew Niccol for Original Screenplay. It lost all three. The film’s last line belongs to two viewers on a couch, asking what else is on.

The lesson

The camera doesn't lie

Henry James described what he was doing

In the prefaces he wrote for the New York Edition of his novels, published 1907 to 1909, Henry James explained his habit of telling a story through one character’s consciousness — what he called a “centre” or a “reflector.” It was a working novelist describing his own method, and he never used the phrase “point of view” for it.

Percy Lubbock turned it into a law

In 1921 the critic Percy Lubbock, who was James’s literary executor, published The Craft of Fiction and made that habit the whole art: “The whole intricate question of method, in the craft of fiction, I take to be governed by the question of the point of view.” The phrase is his, not James’s. From there it became a classroom rule, and eventually “show, don’t tell.” One novelist’s working method hardened into the standard for everyone.

Hollywood’s version: the camera tells the truth

Film grew its own form of the rule. What the camera shows you, happened. In 1950 Alfred Hitchcock broke it in Stage Fright, opening with a flashback that turns out to be a lie. Audiences felt cheated, and he later called it one of his biggest mistakes. Four years later he made Rear Window, where the camera almost never shows you anything Jeff did not see.

Three ways a film can lend you its eyes

One

Restricted

You know only what one character knows, and you find things out when they do.

Rear Window · Memento · Parasite’s first half
Two

Unrestricted

The film shows you more than any character knows, and the tension comes from watching them walk into it.

Psycho · Jaws · The Shining
Three

Subjective

The camera becomes a character’s eyes, for a shot or for a whole film.

Lady in the Lake · Hardcore Henry · found footage

The Truman Show is a fourth case: a film that passes the camera between characters until you keep changing sides.

One piece of vocabulary

Hitchcock separated surprise from suspense using a bomb under a table. If it simply explodes, the audience gets a shock. If they were shown the bomb first, the same scene becomes unbearable: “In the first case we have given the public fifteen seconds of surprise at the moment of the explosion. In the second we have provided them with fifteen minutes of suspense.” The only thing that changed is what the point of view let them know.

Why these films

Chapter 1 was the shape of a story and chapter 2 was its clock. This chapter is the narrator: who is holding the camera, and whether you trust them. One film keeps you in one chair. The other hands you around a room full of watchers until you realise you are one of them.

Rear Window puts you behind the lens. You sit in the dark and watch a life through a window, which is what a cinema is, and what a phone is. The film never settles whether that is a decent impulse or an appetite — only that you will not stop.

The Truman Show turns the apparatus around. Its camera is not with Truman but against him: the few shots that feel like his are cuts the control room chose. It is a show from the first frame to the last, and the wall between the manufactured world and the real one is one pane thick. Rear Window was among the first to suggest we are all a little perverse for looking. The Truman Show finishes the thought — however much you feel for him, you are still Big Brother.
