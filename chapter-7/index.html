Chapter 7 · Character. Who someone is, and whether a story is allowed to change them.

The two roads

Straight on, or a left at the Vista

Hollywood Boulevard runs east until it hits Sunset, and there is a theatre standing on the corner where they meet. Every chapter the room decides at that corner.

A father and a daughter, thirty-one years apart. Francis Ford Coppola’s The Godfather follows a young man who swears he is nothing like his family and becomes the head of it, one of the biggest character transformations in film.

Sofia Coppola’s Lost in Translation follows two strangers in a Tokyo hotel who barely change at all, and trusts that being truly seen is enough of a story. One is the full arc. The other is almost no arc.

Hollywood Blvd. The Godfather Not selected 1972 · 175 min · dir. Francis Ford Coppola · written by Mario Puzo & Francis Ford Coppola · Paramount

The youngest son of a New York crime family wants nothing to do with the business. Then someone tries to kill his father.

Download the The Godfather paper edition (PDF)

Why it is on the ballot. Michael Corleone’s change, from war-hero outsider to the coldest man in the room, is the textbook character arc run in reverse. Every step is visible and every step is a choice. It is three hours long and you will not feel it.

01

Before you press play

Optional trivia.

The studio had to be pushed into both castings

Paramount resisted Marlon Brando, then seen as difficult and box-office poison, and made him screen-test, take low pay and post a bond. Executives wanted Redford, Beatty or Delon for Michael and thought Al Pacino too short. Coppola was close enough to being replaced that he started firing the people angling to replace him. Pacino nearly broke his ankle early in the shoot and was relieved, hoping it might get him fired.

Brando built the face himself

At his home screen test Brando stuffed cotton wool in his cheeks and blacked his hair with shoe polish. For the film he wore a custom dental “plumper” designed by makeup artist Dick Smith and made by a New York dentist. It is now in the collection of the Museum of the Moving Image.

The darkness is a style

Gordon Willis lit many scenes from directly overhead, leaving the characters’ eyes in shadow, an idea that came out of the Brando test: you do not always want to know what this man is thinking. Cinematographers nicknamed him “the Prince of Darkness.” Watch how often you cannot read a face.

02

While you watch

Open each one only when you reach it.

The wedding Where is Michael standing? Open

At his sister’s wedding Michael is in his army uniform, off to the side, explaining his family to his girlfriend like an outsider. “That’s my family, Kay. It’s not me.”

Why it matters: It is the starting point of the arc, and the film puts him at the edge of the frame to say so.

The hospital Steady hands Open

Michael finds his father unguarded and stands watch outside with a terrified baker. Enzo cannot work the lighter; Michael lights it for him, then looks at his own hand.

Why it matters: The other man is shaking. Michael is not. It is the moment he, and you, find out who he really is.

The restaurant The train Open

Michael sits down to dinner with two men. Listen to the elevated train outside getting louder, and notice that the Italian is not subtitled.

Why it matters: No music, no translation. The rising noise is his head. The sound tells you what he has decided before he moves. Paramount only stopped trying to replace Pacino after seeing the dailies from this scene.

The baptism Two things at once Open

Michael stands in a church as godfather to his nephew, renouncing Satan, while the film cuts away to what is happening across the city.

Why it matters: The arc completes inside one sequence, cut by William Reynolds and Peter Zinner. He becomes the Godfather in both senses at the same moment.

03

After the credits

Everything here gives the film away. Keep it for afterwards.

Open The baby in the baptism is Sofia Coppola Open

The infant christened as Michael Francis Rizzi is Francis Ford Coppola’s daughter, who grew up to direct the other film in this chapter.

Open The horse’s head was real Open

It came from a dog-food plant in New Jersey, from a horse that was being put down anyway, and was shipped packed in dry ice. The story that John Marley was not told and screamed for real is not confirmed by any solid source.

Open Eleven nominations became ten, and three wins Open

It won Best Picture, Best Adapted Screenplay and Best Actor. Brando declined the award and sent Sacheen Littlefeather to speak about the treatment of Native Americans in film; the Academy issued her a formal written apology in 2022. Nino Rota’s score was nominated and then withdrawn when the Academy learned the love theme came from his music for Fortunella (1958).

Sunset Blvd. Lost in Translation Not selected 2003 · 102 min · written and directed by Sofia Coppola · Focus Features

A fading American movie star in Tokyo to shoot a whisky ad, and a young woman stuck in the same hotel while her husband works. Neither can sleep, and for one week they keep each other company.

Download the Lost in Translation paper edition (PDF)

Why it is on the ballot. Where The Godfather transforms its hero completely, this film barely moves its characters an inch, and that is the point. Nobody gets a big arc. They get noticed, by each other, and the film asks whether that small shift is enough to count as a story.

01

Before you press play

Optional trivia.

It was written for one actor who never signed anything

Sofia Coppola wrote the part for Bill Murray, who at the time could only be reached through an 800-number answering service. She pursued him for months by phone and letter, flew to Tokyo and spent part of a $4 million budget without knowing whether he would turn up. He turned up.

Twenty-seven days

Principal photography began on 29 September 2002 and ran under a month, mostly in and around the Park Hyatt Tokyo.

Scarlett Johansson was seventeen

She played a recent college graduate while still a teenager. Coppola cast her without an audition.

02

While you watch

Open each one only when you reach it.

The first ride in His face on a billboard Open

Bob arrives in Tokyo, jet-lagged, and passes an advertisement with his own face on it.

Why it matters: The film introduces its character as a stranger to himself. That is the whole arc, already there in the first minute.

The whisky shoot What got lost? Open

The director gives long, passionate instructions in Japanese. The translator gives Bob one short sentence.

Why it matters: It is the title, played as a joke. Most of what these characters feel never makes it across.

Karaoke “More Than This” Open

Bob sings the Roxy Music song to a room of new friends, and Charlotte watches him.

Why it matters: The most revealing moment in the film is a man half-singing a song badly. It trusts small things to carry big feelings.

The last scene The whisper Open

On a crowded street, one character whispers something to the other. You cannot hear it.

Why it matters: The film withholds the one line that would tell you what changed. The character moment belongs to them, not to you.

After the credits

Everything here gives the film away. Keep it for afterwards.

Open Nobody knows what he whispered Open

Murray improvised it, and Coppola chose not to dub it clear — it was meant to stay between the two of them. Fans have been trying to enhance the audio ever since.

Open The songs Open

The closing track is “Just Like Honey” by The Jesus and Mary Chain, and Kevin Shields of My Bloody Valentine wrote original music for the film.

Open One Oscar, from four nominations Open

It won Best Original Screenplay and was nominated for Best Picture, Best Director and Best Actor for Murray — a film with very little plot and some of the most famous silences of its decade.

The lesson

One journey, a thousand faces

A scholar described the world’s myths

In 1949 Joseph Campbell published The Hero with a Thousand Faces, comparing myths from around the world and arguing they share one pattern, the “monomyth” — a term he borrowed from James Joyce. A hero leaves the ordinary world, is tested, is transformed, comes home changed. It was comparative mythology, not a writing manual.

A studio memo made it a formula

George Lucas credited Campbell for Star Wars and called him “my Yoda.” Then, in the mid-1980s, Christopher Vogler, a story analyst at Disney, wrote a seven-page memo called “A Practical Guide to The Hero with a Thousand Faces,” turning Campbell into twelve steps for screenwriters. It circulated through Hollywood, and in 1992 he expanded it into the book The Writer’s Journey. A description of ancient myths became a checklist for development meetings.

The journey, drawn as a circle

This is the hero’s journey — Campbell’s monomyth, the shape Vogler’s memo turned into a checklist. Campbell drew it as a circle, and the circle is the argument: the hero does not finish somewhere new, he finishes where he began, carrying something he did not have when he left. Dan Harmon later compressed the whole thing to eight moves, which is the version below. The line across the middle is the threshold — above it the world he already knows.

Step 1 of 8
Comfort
“A character is in a zone of comfort.” Whatever counts as normal for him, put there so the ending has something to be measured against.
  1. Comfort“A character is in a zone of comfort.” Whatever counts as normal for him, put there so the ending has something to be measured against.
  2. Want“But they want something.” The thing that will not let him stay where he is.
  3. Crossing“They enter an unfamiliar situation.” Past this line the rules are different and he cannot simply go back.
  4. Adapting“Adapt to it.” The new world teaches him what it wants by putting him through it.
  5. Getting it“Get what they wanted.” Usually somewhere near the middle, and never quite the thing he thought he was after.
  6. The price“Pay a heavy price for it.” The low point. Something is lost here, and it is not always a person.
  7. Coming back“Then return to their familiar situation.” The same road, walked the other way, carrying whatever he took.
  8. Changed“Having changed.” Same doorway, same street, same people. The only thing the journey altered is the man walking through it.

Tap any station to read that step.

Eight moves, after Dan Harmon’s compression of Campbell. The quoted lines are his.

The rule it created

The checklist brought an assumption with it: a main character must change. Want something, learn what they really need, come out different. A story where nobody changes started to look, to a development executive, like a story that was not finished.

Three ways a character can move

One

The rise

The character overcomes a flaw and ends up better. The classic hero’s journey.

Star Wars · The Lion King · Moana
Two

The fall

The same steps run downhill. The character gets what they want and loses who they were.

The Godfather · There Will Be Blood · Breaking Bad
Three

The flat line

The character barely changes. Instead the story reveals them, or they change the people around them.

Lost in Translation · Paddington · Paterson

The third one is the hardest to sell and the easiest to love.

One piece of vocabulary

Writers separate a character’s want, what they chase, from their need, what would actually fix them. Michael wants to protect his father. What he needs is to stay out. Watch which one each film cares about.

Why these films

Part Three starts with the person at the centre. A father made the most famous fall in film. His daughter made a film about two people who refuse to transform, and won an Oscar for it.
