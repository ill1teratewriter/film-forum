Chapter 5 · Editing. Editing is film's dark art. Every cut decides what you see, and what you don't.

The two roads

Straight on, or a left at the Vista

Hollywood Boulevard runs east until it hits Sunset, and there is a theatre standing on the corner where they meet. Every chapter the room decides at that corner. This one is watched over Halloween.

Two witch films, one question: when does the film cut to the witch? The Blair Witch Project never does. It pretends nobody edited it at all — three students’ lost tapes — and every cut keeps the thing in the woods out of frame to the last shot.

The Witch is cut like a classic, slow and patient and nearly invisible, and it shows you the witch about ten minutes in. One hides her with the edit. The other reveals her with it. Both want you afraid of the same woods.

Hollywood Blvd. The Blair Witch Project Not selected 1999 · 81 min · written and directed by Daniel Myrick & Eduardo Sánchez · Artisan

Three film students hike into a Maryland forest to make a documentary about a local legend. A year later, their footage is found.

Download the The Blair Witch Project paper edition (PDF)

Why it is on the ballot. It is the film that made found footage a genre, and it is a masterclass in editing that pretends not to be edited. Every cut is disguised as a camera switching off, and almost everything frightening happens in the gaps between shots.

Still online The Blair Witch Project A year before anyone could buy a ticket, this film had a website that never once admitted it was a film. Police reports, a legend, a missing-persons case. People believed it, and it is one of the most enjoyable pieces of marketing anybody has ever run. It is still there, at the address it had in 1999. 01

Before you press play

Optional trivia.

The actors shot it themselves

Heather Donahue, Joshua Leonard and Michael C. Williams, playing characters with their own first names, spent eight days in October 1997 in the woods near Seneca Creek in Montgomery County, Maryland. They operated both cameras. Myrick and Sánchez stayed out of sight and left the next day’s instructions in film cans hidden in milk crates, cutting the food rations as the days went on.

Two cameras, two formats

A CP-16 16mm black-and-white film camera for the “documentary,” and a Hi8 camcorder for the behind-the-scenes video. Neal Fredericks is the credited cinematographer.

It cost almost nothing, by any account

The eight-day shoot has been put at $20,000, $35,000 and $60,000 by the filmmakers themselves in different interviews; the honest version is “well under a hundred thousand dollars,” with post-production and the Sundance finish taking the total somewhere between $200,000 and $750,000. It grossed $248.6 million worldwide.

02

While you watch

Open each one only when you reach it.

Throughout Two cameras, two films Open

One camera shoots grainy black-and-white film. The other is colour video.

Why it matters: Every switch between them is a cut, dressed up as somebody picking up a different camera. Notice which one you are in when things go wrong.

The first night Cut to black Open

At night the film keeps dropping into darkness, sometimes with only sound.

Why it matters: A black screen is an edit. The film is choosing not to show you, and your imagination does the rest — for free, which is roughly what the budget allowed.

The mornings What happened while the camera was off? Open

Things appear outside the tent overnight, and the film skips straight from the night to the discovery.

Why it matters: The horror lives in the missing footage. The edit makes you fill the gap, and you fill it with something worse than they could have shot.

The last shot The corner Open

Remember the story someone tells early on about a man who made children face the corner. Then watch the final seconds.

Why it matters: The film cuts at the exact moment you might finally see something. It is the most famous cut in modern horror, and it shows nothing.

03

After the credits

Everything here gives the film away. Keep it for afterwards.

Open The ending was solved in the cutting room Open

Artisan wanted something more explicit, and the directors shot alternative endings, including Mike hanged and Mike on a stick-figure frame. They threw both away. The corner image came first, because they had no effects money, and the Rustin Parr interview that explains it was added afterwards in the edit so the last shot would land.

Open Around twenty hours became eighty-one minutes Open

The shoot produced roughly 19 to 22 hours of footage, and Myrick and Sánchez cut it themselves over about eight months. A film that looks like nobody touched it is one of the most heavily edited horror films of its decade. Their rule, in Myrick’s words: anything that felt like a movie had to go.

Open The marketing was an edit too Open

From mid-1998 the film’s website presented the disappearance as fact, with police reports and a legend. Days before release the Sci-Fi Channel aired Curse of the Blair Witch, a full mockumentary in which director Ben Rock gave interviewees contradictory backstories, cut their unscripted answers together and artificially aged the “archive” material. The oft-repeated line that IMDb listed the actors as missing or dead is folklore; nobody has produced the page.

Sunset Blvd. The Witch Not selected 2015 · 92 min · written and directed by Robert Eggers · A24 · watch with subtitles on

New England, the 1630s. A Puritan family is banished from their settlement and builds a farm at the edge of a forest. Then the baby disappears.

Download the The Witch paper edition (PDF)

Why it is on the ballot. Where Blair Witch hides everything, this film shows you the witch almost immediately and is still one of the most frightening films of the century. The cutting is patient and nearly invisible, and the fear comes from watching a family fall apart while you already know what is in the woods.

01

Before you press play

Optional trivia.

Turn the subtitles on

Much of the dialogue comes from period journals, diaries and court records — the film says so in its closing titles — and it is spoken in Early Modern English. It is beautiful and hard to catch by ear.

It is a first film, shot in 25 days for about $3.5 million

Robert Eggers’s debut feature and Anya Taylor-Joy’s first leading role. It premiered at Sundance in January 2015, where Eggers won the U.S. Dramatic directing award, and reached cinemas a year later. The full title is stylised The VVitch: A New-England Folktale, the double-V borrowed from Jacobean witchcraft pamphlets.

Lit by the sky and by candles

Shot near Kiosk in remote northern Ontario. Cinematographer Jarin Blaschke used only natural light outdoors, with the schedule bent around keeping the sky overcast, and real candlelight indoors. The darkness is real darkness.

02

While you watch

Open each one only when you reach it.

About ten minutes in Peekaboo Open

A girl plays with the baby at the edge of the trees. Hands over eyes. Hands away.

Why it matters: The film’s whole editing philosophy is in one game. Every cut is a hand over your eyes, and every time the hands come away, something has changed.

Right after The film shows you Open

Where Blair Witch would cut away, this film cuts into the woods and shows you what happened to him.

Why it matters: The chapter’s question is answered in the first act. From here the horror is not whether she is real, it is watching a family who cannot see what you have seen. Eggers calls it “the big early reveal.”

The animals Watch what the edit keeps cutting to Open

A hare. A goat. A raven. The film keeps leaving the family for an animal that is watching them.

Why it matters: An edit can accuse. Every cutaway points at something and asks you to suspect it. Eggers deliberately buried the goat’s importance in the cut — when investors said they could not tell the goat mattered, he said “Good, I don’t want people to know the goat is important.”

The last scene Into the woods Open

After ninety minutes of cutting around the forest, the film finally goes all the way in.

Why it matters: The edit has held the forest at arm’s length the whole time. When it stops, the film is over.

03

After the credits

Everything here gives the film away. Keep it for afterwards.

Open Everything the family fears is real Open

Eggers has described the film as a Puritan nightmare told on its own terms: the witch, the devil and the curse are not metaphors, because to this family they were not. The dialogue and the beliefs come out of the records of the period.

Open The witch is on screen before the family suspects anything Open

The reveal after Samuel vanishes — the knife, the ointment — is an editorial decision, not a horror beat. The film gives away its secret in the first act and then makes you sit with a household of people who do not know it.

Open “Wouldst thou like to live deliciously?” Open

The film’s most quoted line is five words from a goat, and it was everywhere online the year the film came out. Eggers went on to make The Lighthouse, The Northman and Nosferatu.

The lesson

The cut should disappear

Editors worked it out by hand

Through the first half of the twentieth century, Hollywood editors built a set of habits for joining shots so smoothly that nobody noticed the joins. Cut in the middle of a movement. Keep the camera on one side of the action. Match where people are looking. None of it was law. It was craft, passed from editor to assistant.

A British book wrote it down

In 1953 Karel Reisz, working with a committee of the British Film Academy chaired by Thorold Dickinson, published The Technique of Film Editing, which collected what working editors actually did. It became the standard textbook for decades and taught the habits as the rules of continuity editing, with one goal stated plainly: the audience should never notice the cut.

Meanwhile, in Moscow

Thirty years earlier Lev Kuleshov had intercut one neutral close-up of the actor Ivan Mosjoukine with a bowl of soup, a girl in a coffin and a woman on a divan. Audiences praised the actor’s range for hunger, grief and desire. The face never changed; the cut did all the work. Worth knowing that the footage is lost and the story comes to us second-hand through Pudovkin’s 1929 account, and that a 1992 attempt to replicate it failed. The idea survived anyway, because every horror film runs on it.

Three ways to cut

One

Continuity

Smooth, invisible joins that keep you inside the story.

Almost every Hollywood film · The Witch
Two

Collision

Shots slammed together so the meaning comes from the clash rather than from either shot.

Battleship Potemkin · the Psycho shower · Requiem for a Dream
Three

The cut that shows

Jump cuts, black screens, a camera switching off. The edit is on display.

Breathless · The Blair Witch Project · Uncut Gems

A horror film usually runs on the first and the third at once: invisible cutting to keep you inside, and one visible cut to make you jump.

How long is a shot?

Editing has one number attached to it that anybody can check: the average shot length, the runtime divided by the number of shots. It will not tell you whether a film is any good, but it will tell you how hard it is working to hold you, and it separates eras more cleanly than almost anything else you can measure.

The pale notches are published counts. The two in orange are this chapter’s own films, timed in house rather than taken from anyone’s table. Everything bunches between five and nine seconds, which is most of cinema; the room either side of that bunch is where a film is doing something deliberate.

One piece of vocabulary

The Kuleshov effect: viewers read emotion into a shot based on the shot beside it. A face, then the dark, and you decide what the face saw.

Why these films

Chapter 4 decided what goes in the frame. This chapter decides what happens between frames. Blair Witch hides its edits and its witch. The Witch hides neither, and dares you to stay frightened anyway.
