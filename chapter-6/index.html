Chapter 6 · Sound. Half of every film is something you hear, and most of it you never notice.

The two roads

Straight on, or a left at the Vista

Hollywood Boulevard runs east until it hits Sunset, and there is a theatre standing on the corner where they meet. Every chapter the room decides at that corner.

Two crime films with gunfights, getaways and a man who will not stop coming. They sound nothing alike. No Country for Old Men has almost no music: wind, footsteps, a hum from a motel light, the hiss of a gas canister, and silence doing the work a score usually does.

Baby Driver is cut entirely to its soundtrack, with the car turns, the footsteps and even the gunshots landing on the beat of whatever is playing in the hero’s ears. One takes the music away. The other makes everything music.

Hollywood Blvd. No Country for Old Men Not selected 2007 · 122 min · written and directed by Joel & Ethan Coen · from the novel by Cormac McCarthy

A Texas hunter stumbles on a drug deal gone wrong and walks off with two million dollars. A killer with a cattle gun comes after him, and an ageing sheriff trails behind them both.

Download the No Country for Old Men paper edition (PDF)

Why it is on the ballot. It is one of the quietest thrillers ever made and one of the tensest. With almost no score to tell you how to feel, every small sound becomes a threat: a wrapper crinkling, a receiver beeping, footsteps stopping outside a door.

01

Before you press play

Optional trivia.

There is a score. You just will not hear it

Carter Burwell’s own note on the film reads: “Including end credits, there are a mere 16 minutes of music in the film.” He built most of it from singing bowls — metal bowls rubbed to hold one long tone — and worked with the sound team so the music hides inside the effects: “The sounds are snuck in underneath the wind or the sound of a car. When the wind or car goes away, the sound is left behind, but you never hear it appear.”

The silence is designed by hand

Skip Lievsay was supervising sound editor, with Craig Berkey as sound designer and re-recording mixer. The quiet is not an absence of sound work: it is hundreds of small sounds placed so carefully you never catch them being placed.

Listen for the weapon

The killer carries a captive bolt pistol, the air-powered tool used in slaughterhouses, fed by a tank he carries with him. Its hiss and thud is one of the most recognisable sounds in modern film. You will read online that it was built from a nail gun; no interview with the sound team says so, so treat that as rumour.

02

While you watch

Open each one only when you reach it.

The opening Wind Open

An old man talks over empty West Texas landscapes. No music, only wind.

Why it matters: The film tells you in the first minute what it will sound like. You are going to have to listen.

The gas station The coin Open

A killer and a shop owner talk across a counter. Listen to the wrapper, the hum, the pauses.

Why it matters: No music tells you this is the scariest scene in the film. You work it out yourself, which is worse. Burwell tuned the hum under the scene to 60 Hz, the frequency of a refrigerator’s mains buzz, so the music reads as room tone.

The motel The beeping, the light, the footsteps Open

A transponder starts beeping. Shoes block the strip of light under the door. The footsteps stop.

Why it matters: It is a whole action sequence built out of sound. Berkey’s note on it: several things happen off screen early “that force you to focus on the smallest of sounds.”

The last scene No swell Open

The film ends with a man at a kitchen table describing a dream about his father carrying fire through a cold dark pass. “And then I woke up.” Cut to black.

Why it matters: A score would tell you how to feel about that. The Coens refuse, and the silence afterwards is the loudest thing in the film.

After the credits

Everything here gives the film away. Keep it for afterwards.

Open Burwell tried more music and threw it out Open

He experimented with violin harmonics and percussion, and cut them: they “destroyed the tension that came from the quiet.”

Open The Academy noticed the sound Open

Eight nominations, four wins — Picture, Director, Adapted Screenplay and Supporting Actor for Javier Bardem — plus nominations for both Sound Editing and Sound Mixing.

Open The title is a poem, and the date is usually wrong Open

“That is no country for old men” opens W. B. Yeats’s “Sailing to Byzantium,” written in 1926 and first published in October Blast in 1927, before it was collected in The Tower in 1928.

Sunset Blvd. Baby Driver Not selected 2017 · 113 min · written and directed by Edgar Wright · TriStar

A young getaway driver with ringing in his ears drowns it out with music and times every heist to the song in his headphones. Then he falls in love and tries to get out.

Download the Baby Driver paper edition (PDF)

Why it is on the ballot. Where No Country strips the music away, this film is built on top of it. The songs were chosen before the film was shot, played on set, and the editing lands turns, footsteps and gunshots on the beat. It is a musical where nobody sings.

01

Before you press play

Optional trivia.

It started as a music video

In 2003 Edgar Wright directed the video for Mint Royale’s “Blue Song,” in which Noel Fielding plays a getaway driver dancing in his car to the track while Nick Frost, Michael Smiley and Julian Barratt rob a bank — then complains that they were late. Wright: “It’s somewhat similar to the opening scene of Baby Driver.”

Everyone worked to the music

Wright: “We constantly piped the music over the sets so people could act to the song.” Editor Jonathan Amos: “We had very specific points in every track where certain actions had to take place; we called these anchor points.” Paul Machliss cut on location in real time so Wright could see the sequence assembling as they shot it.

The Oscars noticed the cutting and the sound

Nominated for Film Editing (Machliss and Amos), Sound Editing and Sound Mixing, and it won the BAFTA for editing.

02

While you watch

Open each one only when you reach it.

The opening heist Everything is on the beat Open

“Bellbottoms” by The Jon Spencer Blues Explosion. Watch the wipers, the drumming on the wheel, the handbrake turns.

Why it matters: The film teaches you its rule immediately. If it moves, it moves on the beat.

The coffee run One take, and read the walls Open

Baby walks to get coffee in a single unbroken shot to Bob & Earl’s “Harlem Shuffle.” Watch the graffiti and signage he passes: “Move,” “Right,” “Whole Lotta,” “Soul.”

Why it matters: The lyrics are painted on the street. The sound is being turned into picture.

When the music stops Listen to the ringing Open

Every time the earbuds come out you hear what he hears: a high whine. Sound designer Julian Slater pitched the tinnitus to the music on either side of it, so the hum belongs to whatever song just ended.

Why it matters: The film gives you his point of view through sound. The music is not style, it is how he gets through the day.

The shootout Guns on the beat Open

One gunfight is cut to “Tequila” — the Button Down Brass version, not the Champs. The actors had to learn their gunfire like a percussion part, because on set the shots drown out the track.

Why it matters: It is thrilling, and slightly disturbing. Ask whether turning violence into rhythm makes it more fun or harder to watch.

03

After the credits

Everything here gives the film away. Keep it for afterwards.

Open He samples his own life Open

Baby secretly records the people around him and remixes their voices into tracks on cassette. The hero is doing what the film does: cutting the world to a beat.

Open The quiet scenes are in sign language Open

Baby’s deaf foster father Joseph is played by CJ Jones, and their conversations happen in sign. The film’s only real silences are the moments of love.

Open Every sound was tempo-mapped Open

Slater’s team worked out the tempo of the effects themselves so they could be stretched and pitched to sit with the songs: “we knew exactly what the corresponding tempo of those sounds had to be.”

The lesson

Heard, not noticed

Three Soviet directors saw it coming

In 1928, as synchronised sound arrived, Sergei Eisenstein, Vsevolod Pudovkin and Grigori Alexandrov published a short “Statement on Sound.” They warned that sound would be used for “talking films” — recorded theatre — which would destroy the art of the cut, and argued for the opposite: “Only a contrapuntal use of sound in relation to the visual montage piece will afford a new potentiality of montage development and perfection.”

Hollywood did the opposite

The studios synchronised everything — every line, every door, every footstep — and composers like Max Steiner laid a lush orchestral score underneath that followed the feeling of every scene. The working rule became: the audience should feel the music, not hear it. A good score was one nobody noticed.

The rule got its name later

In 1987 the scholar Claudia Gorbman set out the principles of classical Hollywood scoring in Unheard Melodies. One of them is inaudibility: music is not meant to be heard consciously. She was describing a habit, not prescribing one, but the phrase stuck, and the habit is still the default in most films you watch.

Three ways a film can use sound

One

Match it

Sound follows the picture and the score follows the feeling. You never think about it.

Almost everything
Two

Take it away

Little or no score, so small sounds and silence carry the tension.

No Country for Old Men · A Quiet Place · The Birds
Three

Let it drive

The music comes first and the picture is cut to it.

Baby Driver · Whiplash · Guardians of the Galaxy

The third one is rare because it costs money: the songs have to be cleared before a frame is shot.

Two pieces of vocabulary

Diegetic sound exists inside the story — the characters can hear it. Non-diegetic sound, like most scores, is only for you. Almost all of Baby Driver’s music is diegetic, because Baby is playing it. Room tone is the sound of a space when nobody is talking; every set records it, and No Country is practically built out of it.

Why these films

This closes Part Two. The frame decides what you see, the cut decides when, and sound decides how it feels. One film proves how loud silence is. The other proves a film can be edited like a song.
