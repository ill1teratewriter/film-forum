The two roads

Straight on, or a left at the Vista

Hollywood Boulevard runs east until it hits Sunset, and there is a theatre standing on the corner where they meet. Every chapter the room decides at that corner, and this one went straight on.

Hollywood Blvd. Voted Field guide Dunkirk 2017 · 106 min · written and directed by Christopher Nolan · Warner Bros. Won 3 Academy Awards in 2018: Film Editing, Sound Editing, Sound Mixing

Four hundred thousand men are pinned on a beach in northern France with the sea in front of them and the German army closing behind. The film follows the escape from three places at once, on the sand, on the water and in the air, and each of the three is running for a different length of time.

01

Before you press play

Optional trivia.

The script was 76 pages

The industry rule of thumb is roughly a page a minute, so 76 pages should give you a 76 minute film. This one runs 106. The gap is the whole character of the thing, because what fills those extra thirty minutes is action rather than talk.

Dialogue was hard to shoot

IMAX 70mm cameras are enormous and extremely loud, which normally rules them out for anything with talking in it. Nolan wanted the format more than he wanted the conversations, so the format won, and what he ended up making is close to a silent film built in the twenty first century.

Hans Zimmer recorded Nolan's watch

The ticking under the score is a real recording of one of Nolan's own pocket watches, run through synthesisers. Laid over the top is a Shepard tone, an audio illusion of a note that rises forever without ever actually getting higher.

02

While you watch

Four moments, framed so the ticking clock conversation happens in real time while you are still watching rather than afterwards.

First two minutes The film tells you the rule out loud Open

Three title cards, one for each strand. The Mole, one week. The Sea, one day. The Air, one hour. The whole structure is handed to you before anything has happened, in typography, before a single character has been introduced.

Throughout Has this already happened? Open

Three stories are running at three different speeds. So every time the film cuts, ask yourself one question, and keep asking it.

Is this happening now, or has it already happened?

The air When the pilot starts writing on his own dashboard Open

Early in the air strand an instrument stops working, and the pilot begins keeping track by hand, in chalk, on the panel in front of him. From that moment the film has a countdown you can read with your own eyes, attached to a man who cannot stop it or refill it.

It is the clearest clock in the film, and it is a piece of chalk.

The last reel Does the clock ever stop? Open

The film has been ticking at you for an hour and a half.

Does the clock stop at any point? And if it does, why there?

03

After you watch

Everything below gives away how the film was made. Keep it for after the credits, or print the paper edition and read it on the way home.

Location It was shot on the actual beach Open

Nolan took the production to Dunkirk itself and filmed on the real sand at Malo-les-Bains. The Mole you see is a genuine stone jetty at the western end of that beach, rebuilt for the film with the wooden superstructure it carried in 1940.

The rest went to places that still look like 1940. Weymouth Harbour in Dorset stood in for the departure point, chosen because its Georgian waterfront had barely changed. The controlled water work went to the IJsselmeer near Urk in the Netherlands, and the interiors were shot on the Universal backlot lake and in Warner Bros Stage 16, which holds a two million gallon tank.

Practical Real ships, real Spitfires, and some cardboard Open

Twenty of the original Little Ships that were actually at Dunkirk in 1940 were brought back for the film. The Spitfires are real Spitfires, and Nolan put IMAX cameras in the aircraft rather than building the dogfights digitally.

Where the crowds needed to look bigger than the several thousand extras on hand, the production filled the distance with cardboard cutouts. Chapter one had little people standing in as mechanics to fake the depth of an airfield in 1942. Same instinct, seventy five years apart.

Numbers What it cost and what it won Open

A hundred million dollar budget, against 530 million worldwide, which made it the highest grossing film about the Second World War until Nolan passed himself with Oppenheimer in 2023.

Eight Academy Award nominations including Best Picture and Best Director, and three wins. Film Editing, Sound Editing and Sound Mixing. Every award it took was for time and rhythm, which is worth sitting with for a second given what the film is about.

The film tells you its structure in the first two minutes. Would it be a better film if it hid that, or would it fall apart?

Sunset Blvd. Run Lola Run Not selected 1998 · 80 min · dir. Tom Tykwer · German

Lola has twenty minutes to find a hundred thousand marks and save her boyfriend, and the film runs those same twenty minutes three separate times. It is the loop in its purest commercial form, where the clock resets instead of running out, so the tension stops being about whether time runs out and becomes about what changes on the next pass.

Download the Run Lola Run paper edition (PDF)

Why it was on the ballot

Against Dunkirk it makes the sharpest possible pair. One film runs three clocks of different lengths at once so they finish together. The other runs one clock three times over. Both are asking whether a story is a fixed shape or just one route through a much larger set of them.

Three facts, no spoilers

  • Twenty minutes, stretched to eighty. Tykwer has said the film started from an image of a woman running, and the plan was to invert the usual proportion of a feature, taking roughly twenty minutes of action and letting it fill four times that length. The finished film runs eighty minutes.
  • The director is one of the three people who wrote the music. The score is credited to Tom Tykwer, Johnny Klimek and Reinhold Heil, which is a rare arrangement, and it shows in how tightly the cutting sits on the beat.
  • Two million dollars, and it opened America to German film. Made for about two million, it took more than seven million in North America, which at the time put it among the ten highest grossing foreign language releases the United States had ever had. Audience Award at Sundance in 1999, and an Independent Spirit Award.

While you watch

Four moments, framed the same way as the other road, so the ticking clock conversation happens while you are still watching.

First three minutes It tells you the rule twice before it starts Open

Two quotations arrive before anything moves. One is T. S. Eliot, we shall not cease from exploration, and the end of all our exploring will be to arrive where we started. The other is the German national football coach Sepp Herberger, after the game is before the game. Then a security guard talks straight down the lens, a football goes up, and the title comes out of the crowd.

A film about repetition opens by quoting a poet and a football manager saying the same thing in two different registers.

Whenever Lola is off screen The picture quality changes Open

Lola was shot on 35mm. A great deal of what happens away from her was shot on video, and some of what has already happened comes back as grainy black and white. Watch the image firm up the moment she walks into a room, and go soft and cheap again when she leaves it.

The format is doing character work. Worth asking whether the film thinks she is the only one in it who is fully alive.

The stairwell She stops being a person and becomes a drawing Open

Every run starts the same way, down the same stairs, and the film hands that stretch to animation, where a drawn Lola runs a corridor of clock faces. It happens three times, and it is not identical three times.

The drawing is where the film tips its hand earliest, before anyone on screen knows the run has changed.

Every stranger she touches The photographs Open

Brush past somebody and the film stops dead and deals out a handful of stills of that person's entire future, in about two seconds. Same people every run, different futures each time, and they go by fast enough to miss.

This is the film's real argument, made in the margins rather than in the plot.

If you took this road

  • Does anything carry over? Lola starts each run from the same doorway. Decide whether she is learning, or whether the film is simply dealing the cards again, and notice which answer makes the ending land harder.
  • Whose clock is it? Nothing in the film is actually counting down on screen the way Dunkirk counts. So work out where the twenty minutes is being kept, and what is standing in for a clock.
03 · The lesson

The oldest rule in the book

Aristotle described a habit

Writing about Greek tragedy around 335 BC, Aristotle noticed that a tragedy "tries as hard as it can to exist during a single daylight period, or to vary but little." He is describing a tendency in plays that already existed, the way you would say most pop songs run about three minutes, which is taking notes rather than issuing instructions.

Italy turned it into a law

In 1570, an Italian scholar named Lodovico Castelvetro published a translation of the Poetics with his own commentary attached, and in that commentary the observation hardened into a requirement, that a play must happen inside a single day. Joined with unity of place and unity of action it became the three unities, and it went out under Aristotle's name rather than Castelvetro's.

France enforced it for a century and a half

French classical theatre made the unities the standard of serious drama, argued them in public, and treated the writers who ignored them as amateurs. For roughly a hundred and fifty years the most influential theatre in Europe ran on a deadline one man inferred from another man's notes.

Chapter one found the three act structure published as a rulebook in 1979, thirty seven years after Casablanca was shot without one. Same move, four hundred years earlier, in the same book.

Three ways a film can run a clock

What actually separates them is the relationship between story time, meaning how long the events take, and screen time, meaning how long you sit there. Dunkirk is doing more than one at once.

One

The deadline

A time inside the story that somebody has to beat. Screen time stays free to compress or stretch, and the film cuts across the boring parts while the deadline holds.

High Noon · Apollo 13 · Speed
Two

Real time

Screen time and story time are locked to the same length. The film cannot cut forward, so it has to fill every minute it claims.

The Set-Up · Rope · Cléo from 5 to 7 · 24
Three

The loop

The same span of story time runs more than once, so the clock resets instead of running out, and the tension moves to what changes on the next pass.

Groundhog Day · Source Code · Edge of Tomorrow

The fourth case is running more than one of these at once, which is what Dunkirk is doing.

One piece of vocabulary

Writers separate a time lock from an option lock. A time lock ends the story when the clock runs out. An option lock ends it when the character runs out of things to try, which can feel identical from the seat even though nothing is counting down. Worth deciding, somewhere in the second half, which one you are actually watching.

Why this is the film for it

Dunkirk is running three of these at the same time, at three different lengths, which means every cut is asking you to hold a position in three separate countdowns at once. Chapter one was about the shape a story is supposed to have. This one is about what happens when a film stops using shape to organise itself and starts using duration instead.
