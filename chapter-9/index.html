Chapter 9 · Genre. Genre is the promise a film makes before it starts. The shape comes after.

The two roads

Straight on, or a left at the Vista

Hollywood Boulevard runs east until it hits Sunset, and there is a theatre standing on the corner where they meet. Every chapter the room decides at that corner. This is the last one.

Two sets of outlaws running for the Mexican border. Both films make the same promise inside twenty minutes: this is a crime film, and somebody is going to pay for it. Thelma & Louise keeps that promise to the last frame.

From Dusk Till Dawn keeps it for about an hour, reaches Mexico, and then becomes a different film altogether, with no warning and no apology. One honours the contract. The other tears it up halfway through and never looks back. Harvey Keitel is in both.

Hollywood Blvd. Thelma & Louise Not selected 1991 · 130 min · dir. Ridley Scott · written by Callie Khouri · MGM

Two friends leave for a weekend away from their small lives in Arkansas. One night at a roadside bar changes everything, and they keep driving.

Download the Thelma & Louise paper edition (PDF)

Why it is on the ballot. It takes the oldest American genre promise — outlaws on the run — hands it to two women, and then keeps it with total conviction. It never cheats and never swerves, and it arrives exactly where its genre was always heading.

01

Before you press play

Optional trivia.

It was a first script, and it won the Oscar

Callie Khouri was working in music videos when she wrote it. It was her first screenplay, and it won Best Original Screenplay. The film took six nominations in five categories, with Susan Sarandon and Geena Davis both nominated for Best Actress, plus Ridley Scott for directing, Adrian Biddle for cinematography and Thom Noble for editing.

Look out for a young Brad Pitt

J.D. first went to William Baldwin, who dropped out. George Clooney, Christian Slater, Robert Downey Jr., Mark Ruffalo and Dermot Mulroney were among those who read for it. Geena Davis screen-tested with several of them and recommended Pitt.

The West is Utah and California

The story runs through Arkansas and Oklahoma, but almost none of it was shot there. The road belongs to rural California, Utah and Colorado, and the red-rock country is the land above the Colorado River near Moab, Utah — not the Grand Canyon it is so often taken for.

02

While you watch

Open each one only when you reach it.

Before they leave The Polaroid Open

The two women take a snapshot of themselves before they set off.

Why it matters: It is who they were before the genre got hold of them. Keep that photo in mind all the way down the road.

The parking lot The promise is made Open

About twenty minutes in, one moment turns a weekend comedy into a crime film.

Why it matters: This is the contract. From this second the film owes you an outlaw story, and everything that follows pays it.

Louise’s rule Not through Texas Open

Louise refuses the direct route to Mexico because it goes through Texas, and will not say why.

Why it matters: The genre is pulling them south and her past is pulling against it. The route becomes the character.

The last scene Tragedy or getaway? Open

Surrounded at the edge of a canyon, they make a choice, and the film freezes.

Why it matters: The freeze-frame and the fade to white hand the decision to you. Bring your answer on Sunday.

03

After the credits

Everything here gives the film away. Keep it for afterwards.

Open The ending had to be protected Open

A longer version was shot and exists on home video: the car falls, Hal walks to the wreck, the helicopter arrives. Scott also considered and rejected a version in which Louise pushes Thelma clear. Khouri has said Scott “really fought for that ending,” and his right to shoot it was written into his contract, because the studio kept asking for something else. Roger Ebert thought the fade to white honoured the tragedy and the credits montage took it back.

Open Harvey Keitel plays the only man on their side Open

Detective Hal Slocumb is the one character trying to bring them in alive. Five years later Keitel turned up in From Dusk Till Dawn as a preacher driving his family toward Mexico.

Open It became a national argument Open

Time put it on the cover on 24 June 1991 under the line “Why Thelma & Louise Strikes a Nerve.” Hans Zimmer scored it, with Pete Haycock’s slide guitar on the main theme.

Sunset Blvd. From Dusk Till Dawn Not selected 1996 · 108 min · dir. Robert Rodriguez · screenplay by Quentin Tarantino · Dimension Films

Two bank-robbing brothers take a preacher and his kids hostage and run for the Mexican border, where they plan to lie low until dawn at a roadside bar.

Download the From Dusk Till Dawn paper edition (PDF)

Why it is on the ballot. For its first hour it is a tight, nasty Tarantino crime film. Then it becomes something else entirely, with no warning and no apology. It is the cleanest example of a film deliberately breaking the promise its genre made, and daring you to keep watching.

01

Before you press play

Optional trivia.

It was Tarantino’s first paid writing job

Robert Kurtzman’s makeup-effects company KNB paid him $1,500 in 1990 to write the script, years before Reservoir Dogs. It was commissioned by an effects house rather than a studio, which tells you something about what they were paying for.

It was George Clooney’s jump to film

His first major film lead, shot while he was still starring as Doug Ross on ER. Tarantino plays his brother, Richie.

Harvey Keitel is in both films this chapter

In Thelma & Louise he is the detective. Here he is Jacob Fuller, a pastor who has lost his faith, driving his kids across the border in an RV. Cheech Marin plays three roles: the border guard, Chet Pussy the barker, and Carlos.

02

While you watch

Open each one only when you reach it.

The first scene The promise Open

A liquor store, a sheriff, two brothers. Pure crime film, and very good at it.

Why it matters: The film makes its genre promise as loudly as it can. Remember how sure you felt about what kind of movie this was.

The border The RV Open

The brothers and their hostages try to get through the checkpoint.

Why it matters: This is the last stretch where the crime story is the whole story. Watch it knowing that.

The bar Clock the minute Open

Check the time when the film changes. It lands somewhere around 55 to 60 minutes in.

Why it matters: The switch arrives almost exactly where a classic structure would put the midpoint. The film breaks its genre and keeps its structure in the same move. Roger Ebert’s summary: “For about an hour, we’ve been following the story of two mad-dog killers and their victims, and then suddenly a stripper in a Mexican bar turns into a vampire, and off we go.”

The last shot Pull back Open

As the survivors drive away, the camera pulls back behind the bar.

Why it matters: The bar is sitting on the exposed top of a buried Mesoamerican step temple, its base ringed by decades of wrecked trucks. One reveal re-labels the whole second half: the trap is centuries old.

03

After the credits

Everything here gives the film away. Keep it for afterwards.

Open The snake was not in the script Open

Salma Hayek is genuinely afraid of snakes and refused the dance until she was told they would simply cast someone else — “frankly, I really needed to pay the rent.” She got through it by treating the snake as a symbol of her own power.

Open The money Open

Made for about $19 million, it took roughly $26 million in the United States and about $59 million worldwide, then became a franchise: two direct-to-video follow-ups and a television series from 2014 to 2016. The genre switch became the brand.

Open The cast is a horror hall of fame Open

Tom Savini, the makeup-effects legend, plays Sex Machine. Fred Williamson, the blaxploitation star, plays Frost. Juliette Lewis plays Kate Fuller. The film is a crime picture staffed by horror people, which is the joke it is about to tell you.

The lesson

Nobody called it noir

A French critic named something he saw

After the Second World War, American films that had been kept out of occupied France arrived in Paris all at once: The Maltese Falcon, Double Indemnity, Laura, Murder, My Sweet. On 28 August 1946, in L’Écran français, the Italian-born critic Nino Frank wrote them up under the heading of a new kind of crime picture and called the mood film noir. The people who made those films in Hollywood had never heard the phrase. To them they were crime pictures.

Americans made it a genre

In the spring of 1972 the screenwriter Paul Schrader published “Notes on Film Noir” in Film Comment, and a generation of American directors started making noir deliberately: Chinatown (1974), Body Heat (1981), Blade Runner (1982). A label invented after the fact to describe a mood became a style you could aim at, with a checklist: hard shadows, voiceover, flashbacks, a doomed hero, a dangerous woman.

That is how genre works

Most genres are born this way. The films come first, the name comes later, and then the name becomes a promise to the audience. Once you know the name, you know what you have been promised: a western ends in a showdown, a romantic comedy ends together, a crime film makes somebody pay.

Three things a film can do with the promise

One

Keep it

Deliver what the genre promised, as well as it can be delivered.

Die Hard · Heat · Thelma & Louise
Two

Bend it

Stay inside the genre while playing with what you expect.

Scream · Knives Out · Get Out
Three

Break it

Switch genres partway through and dare you to follow.

Psycho · Audition · From Dusk Till Dawn

A genre is really a preset of everything in this book: a structure, a clock, a point of view, a frame, a cut, a sound, a kind of character and a kind of performance, all agreed before the lights go down.

Where a genre comes from

Nobody sits down and invents a genre. The films turn up first, somebody notices they rhyme, and a name arrives afterwards — often from a critic, sometimes decades late. Then the name starts working backwards, and films get made to fit it. Which is why the machine below claims nothing about what came first. It takes the five most used genres, cuts each into five of the sub-genres you are most likely to meet, and drops a ball down each.

Comedy

  1. SlapstickBodies and objects rebel. The pratfall, the chase and the collision tell the joke, not the dialogue.First L’Arroseur arrosé, 1895 — forty-five seconds, and the first fiction film anyone made.Most watched Home Alone, 1990 — $477m.
  2. ScrewballTwo mismatched people insult each other into love, fast, and usually across a class line.First Disputed — It Happened One Night and Twentieth Century, both 1934. The word arrived in 1936.Most watched It Happened One Night, 1934 — no gross survives; it swept all five top Oscars.
  3. ParodyCopies one film, or one whole genre, closely enough to be recognised, and then punctures it.First Usually The Little Train Robbery, 1905 — children robbing a train two years after the real one. Thinly evidenced.Most watched Austin Powers in Goldmember, 2002 — $296m.
  4. Gross-outAimed at the gag reflex. Bodily fluids, taboo and bad taste, all played for the laugh.First Disputed — Pink Flamingos, 1972, is the archetype; the press first used the phrase for Animal House, 1978.Most watched The Hangover, 2009 — $466m.
  5. MockumentaryFiction shot and cut as a documentary, so that the form itself is the joke.First Disputed — David Holzman’s Diary, 1967, or Take the Money and Run, 1969. Spinal Tap, 1984, only fixed it.Most watched Borat, 2006 — $262m.

Drama

  1. MelodramaEmotion at maximum. Virtue suffers, fate is cruel, and the score tells you exactly what to feel.First None — it walked in from the nineteenth-century stage already finished. Griffith set its film shape, Way Down East, 1920.Most watched Gone with the Wind, 1939 — $3.4bn adjusted, still the largest audience ever sold.
  2. CourtroomThe story happens in one room, where talk is the action and a verdict is the clock.First Unsettled — Falsely Accused!, 1908, is the earliest title cited and barely documented. The Passion of Joan of Arc, 1928, is the earliest anyone screens.Most watched A Few Good Men, 1992 — $243m.
  3. Coming-of-ageOne summer, or one year, in which a young person crosses a line and cannot go back over it.First Nobody names one. Zéro de conduite, 1933, Rebel Without a Cause, 1955, and The 400 Blows, 1959, are all put forward.Most watched Grease, 1978 — $396m.
  4. Social problemPuts a named injustice on trial through one ordinary person who has to live inside it.First Traffic in Souls, 1913 — “melodrama, social indictment and documentary” at once, says the Library of Congress.Most watched Erin Brockovich, 2000 — $258m.
  5. BiopicOne real life compressed into a shape: the rise, the cost, the moment they became the name.First Jeanne d’Arc, 1900, Méliès — eleven minutes, and the earliest film anyone files as a biopic.Most watched Michael, 2026 — the first biopic past a billion.

Action

  1. SwashbucklerSword-fighting adventure with a grinning hero who leaps, duels and rescues. Never grim.First The Mark of Zorro, 1920 — Douglas Fairbanks more or less wrote the job description.Most watched Pirates of the Caribbean: Dead Man’s Chest, 2006 — $1.07bn.
  2. Martial artsThe fights are the storytelling: trained bodies, choreography, and a code the hero has to earn.First The Burning of the Red Lotus Temple, 1928 — Shanghai, silent, and eighteen sequels.Most watched The Karate Kid, 2010 — $359m.
  3. SpyCover identities and betrayal. The weapon is information, and nobody is who they say they are.First None settled — British shorts of 1913 and 1914 come first; Lang’s Spione, 1928, set the moves still in use.Most watched Skyfall, 2012 — $1.11bn.
  4. DisasterA crowd of strangers against something overwhelming. The question is who survives, not who did it.First Deluge, 1933 — lost for fifty years, and found again in 1981.Most watched Titanic, 1997 — $2.26bn.
  5. HeistA crew assembles, plans an impossible robbery in detail, does it, and then the plan comes apart.First Disputed — The Asphalt Jungle, 1950, or Rififi, 1955.Most watched Ocean’s Eleven, 2001 — $451m.

Horror

  1. GothicDread built out of place and inheritance: an old house, a family curse, and a building that seems to want something.First Disputed — Le Manoir du diable, 1896, has the furniture; gothic cinema proper is usually dated to Caligari, 1920, or Nosferatu, 1922.Most watched Bram Stoker’s Dracula, 1992 — $216m. Gothic is a mood, not a box-office category.
  2. VampireAn undead predator who has to be invited in, feeds on blood, and turns attraction into something catching.First Nosferatu, 1922 — the earliest that survives. Drakula halála, 1921, came first, but the film is lost and nobody can say what was in it.Most watched Breaking Dawn – Part 2, 2012 — $868m.
  3. CreatureOne oversized animal or monster hunts people while the authorities stand around arguing about it.First Disputed — The Lost World, 1925, for stop motion; King Kong, 1933, for the template; Godzilla, 1954, for the man in the suit.Most watched Jurassic World, 2015 — $1.67bn. Keep it to the B-picture strand and it is Jaws, 1975 — $495m.
  4. PossessionA body is occupied by something intelligent and evil, and faith rather than force is the only weapon that works.First Not The Exorcist — possession films come before 1973. But it wrote the grammar every one since has used.Most watched The Exorcist, 1973 — $431m across its releases.
  5. SlasherA masked killer works through a group of young people one at a time, and one of them is left to face him.First Nobody agrees. Psycho and Peeping Tom, both 1960; A Bay of Blood, 1971; Black Christmas, 1974; Halloween, 1978, fixed the formula.Most watched Halloween, 2018 — $260m.

Science fiction

  1. Outer spaceGetting off the Earth is the engine of the story, and the vacuum supplies the danger.First Le Voyage dans la Lune, 1902, Méliès — with his own earlier trick films laying a claim.Most watched Star Wars: The Force Awakens, 2015 — $2.07bn.
  2. AliensSomething not human makes contact, and the real subject is whether we answer with curiosity or a weapon.First Le Voyage dans la Lune, 1902 — the Selenites are the first aliens anybody filmed.Most watched Avatar, 2009 — $2.92bn.
  3. ApocalypseCivilisation has already fallen. The film is about what the survivors eat, build and turn into.First Unsettled — Verdens Undergang, 1916, for the fall itself; Deluge, 1933, and Five, 1951, for what comes after, though that claim is asserted more often than sourced.Most watched I Am Legend, 2007 — $586m.
  4. CyberpunkA near future run by corporations, where bodies and minds can be hacked and the hero is a low-life specialist.First Blade Runner, 1982 — four years older than the word. Metropolis, 1927, is named as an ancestor, not an origin.Most watched The Matrix Reloaded, 2003 — $742m.
  5. FantasyMagic, myth or another world running on its own rules, and nobody in the film asks for a scientific explanation.First No settled credit. Everyone points at Méliès’s trick films — Le Manoir du diable, 1896, then Cendrillon, 1899.Most watched Ne Zha 2, 2025 — $2.22bn.

Twenty-five bins, every one of them a sub-genre on the chart the club works from — which is also why Fantasy is a bin inside Science Fiction rather than a lane of its own. Where the first film is genuinely argued over the bin says so rather than picking a winner, which turns out to be most of them. The last two lanes are what Europe files together as fantastic cinema: horror, science fiction and fantasy as one family, the word Sitges has used since 1968 and the festivals federation took for its name. They arrive together too. Méliès was making all three between 1896 and 1902, and which of them came first is an argument about labels nobody had invented yet.

One piece of vocabulary

A genre switch is a deliberate change of genre inside one film. It works when the first promise has been kept so well that the break feels like a betrayal, and then gives you something worth being betrayed for.

Why these films, and why last

Chapter 1 was the shape of a story. This chapter is the promise made before the shape begins. So the season ends where it started, with the rules: two films, one that keeps them to the end and one that breaks them halfway through, and arrives anyway. Next time you are in a theatre, ask which one the film is doing.
