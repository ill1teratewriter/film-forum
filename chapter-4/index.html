Chapter 4 · Frame. Where the film puts things, and what it leaves out.

The two roads

Straight on, or a left at the Vista

Hollywood Boulevard runs east until it hits Sunset, and there is a theatre standing on the corner where they meet. Every chapter the room decides at that corner.

Two filmmakers who never put anything in the frame by accident, using that control for opposite reasons. Wes Anderson puts the most important thing dead centre and builds the world around it like a dollhouse. Wong Kar-wai pushes his lovers to the edges, behind doors, into corridors and mirrors, and leaves the middle of the frame empty.

One frame shows you everything. The other hides half of it. Both are breaking the same rule, from opposite sides.

Hollywood Blvd. The Grand Budapest Hotel Not selected 2014 · 99 min · written and directed by Wes Anderson · Fox Searchlight

A legendary hotel concierge and his teenage lobby boy are framed for murder in a made-up European country, just as war arrives. A caper, a prison break and a ski chase, told as a story inside a story inside a story.

Download the The Grand Budapest Hotel paper edition (PDF)

Why it is on the ballot. No filmmaker working today is more recognisable from a single frame. Anderson centres, balances and flattens every shot, and in this film he changes the actual shape of the screen three times. It is the easiest film in the world to pause and study.

01

Before you press play

Optional trivia.

The screen changes shape three times

Anderson and cinematographer Robert Yeoman shot three ratios for three eras: the near-square Academy frame, 1.37:1, for 1932; wide 2.35:1 anamorphic for 1968; and standard 1.85:1 for the 1985 and present-day bookends. Watch the black bars at the edge of your screen move.

The hotel is a model, and the lobby is a department store

The pink hotel on the hill is a 1:18-scale miniature built by Atelier Simon Weisse in Berlin. The lobby was built inside a disused 1912 department store in Görlitz, Germany, near the Polish border, which stood in for most of the fictional country.

It is dedicated to a writer

The credits say the film was “inspired by the writings of Stefan Zweig,” an Austrian novelist hugely popular before the Second World War who fled Europe as it collapsed. No single book is adapted.

02

While you watch

Open each one only when you reach it.

The first jump back in time The bars close in Open

When the film arrives in 1932 the picture shrinks to a near square.

Why it matters: The frame itself tells you what year it is. The past is literally narrower. Anderson has said the taller frame also “sees ceilings a lot more,” which is why the 1932 interiors feel capped and boxed.

Any lobby scene Find the centre Open

Pick any shot of Gustave in the hotel and look at the middle of the screen. Something important is almost always sitting there: a face, a door, a lift, a cake.

Why it matters: Centred frames feel controlled and slightly unreal, like a stage. That is exactly who Gustave is.

Whenever the camera moves Straight lines only Open

The camera whip-pans hard left or right, tilts straight up or down, or tracks sideways. It almost never drifts.

Why it matters: Moving in right angles keeps the frame flat, like the pages of a picture book.

The ski chase The film admits it is a toy Open

The mountain chase mixes stop-motion figures, miniature bobsled runs and model gondolas with matte paintings, and does not hide it.

Why it matters: The film is honest about being made. Ask whether that makes the danger feel smaller, or more like a memory.

03

After the credits

Everything here gives the film away. Keep it for afterwards.

Open Four storytellers, three frames Open

A girl at the Author’s monument in the present, the Author in 1985, the young Author at the hotel in 1968, and Zero’s story in 1932. Each layer sits in its own period, and the aspect ratio tracks it.

Open The square frame is a piece of real history Open

The 1932 ratio is the Academy aperture, adopted by the American studios in 1931 and codified by the Academy the following year. Anderson shot the past in the shape the past was actually shot in.

Open Four Oscars, all for building the world Open

From nine nominations it won Production Design, Costume Design, Makeup and Hairstyling, and Alexandre Desplat’s score. Nothing for the acting or the script. The awards went to the frame.

01

Everything is centred and symmetrical. Did that make you feel calm, or trapped? Is there a moment where it stops working?

02

The frame gets wider as the story gets later. What does the film think happened to the world between 1932 and 1968?

03

Is the prettiness hiding what the film is about, or is it how the film grieves?

Sunset Blvd. In the Mood for Love Not selected 2000 · 98 min · dir. Wong Kar-wai · Cantonese · watch with subtitles on

Hong Kong, 1962. Two neighbours in a crowded apartment building discover that their husband and wife are having an affair with each other, and slowly, carefully, fall into something they refuse to call love.

Download the In the Mood for Love paper edition (PDF)

Why it is on the ballot. Where Anderson centres everything, Wong hides it. His two leads are squeezed into doorways, cut off by walls, caught in mirrors and through windows. The frame keeps them apart even when they are standing next to each other, and the empty space says what they will not.

01

Before you press play

Optional trivia.

There was no finished script

Wong shot on and off for roughly fifteen months, handing actors pages on the morning of the shoot, and cut a great deal at the end — including an intimate scene between the two leads. He has said he would cut them again: the film is about absence.

Old Hong Kong was already gone

Significant parts of it — street exteriors, corridors, hotel material — were shot in older districts of Bangkok, because by 2000 almost nothing in Hong Kong still looked like the Hong Kong of 1962.

Critics now rank it among the greatest films ever made

In the 2022 Sight and Sound poll, voted by 1,639 critics, programmers and academics, it placed fifth of all time, up from 24th ten years earlier. Tony Leung won Best Actor at Cannes for it in 2000.

02

While you watch

Open each one only when you reach it.

Throughout Count the frames inside the frame Open

Doorways, window bars, mirrors, stairwell railings, curtains. Most shots of the two of them are taken through something.

Why it matters: They are always watched, always boxed in. In a building this crowded, every conversation has an audience, and the framing makes you part of it.

The husband and the wife Where are their faces? Open

You hear the two spouses and see the backs of their heads. The film never shows either face.

Why it matters: The frame decides who matters. The people having the affair are left out of their own story.

The walk to the noodle stall Slow motion, same music, different dress Open

She goes down the same steps with the same tin, to the same waltz, several times over. The dresses, all designed and sourced by William Chang — who was also the production designer and the editor — are how you tell the passes apart.

Why it matters: The only sign that weeks are going by is her wardrobe. The film tells time with costume and framing, not dialogue.

The rehearsals Is this real? Open

At one point she confronts her husband, and it is not what it seems.

Why it matters: The framing lets you believe it before the film tells you what you are actually looking at. The camera keeps secrets too.

03

After the credits

Everything here gives the film away. Keep it for afterwards.

Open Room 2046 Open

The hotel room where they meet to write is number 2046. Wong’s next film, 2046, returns to the same man years later.

Open The music is borrowed Open

The waltz over every slow walk is “Yumeji’s Theme” by Shigeru Umebayashi, written for Seijun Suzuki’s Yumeji in 1991. Three Nat King Cole songs in Spanish carry the rest, including “Quizás, Quizás, Quizás.”

Open The ending is a whisper you cannot hear Open

In 1966, far from Hong Kong, a man speaks a secret into a hollow in a wall at Angkor Wat and seals it with mud. The film frames the moment and never lets you hear it. It is the last thing it chooses to leave outside the frame.

01

The film almost never puts the two of them together in the centre of the frame. What did that do to you?

02

You never see the spouses’ faces. Did you want to, and what would it have changed?

03

Anderson’s frame shows you everything. Wong’s hides half of it. Which one told you more?

The lesson

Divide by three

An engraver described a habit

In 1797 an English engraver named John Thomas Smith published Remarks on Rural Scenery, about painting landscapes. He noticed painters tended to divide a picture in proportions of about two thirds to one third — how much sky against how much land — and named it, with an apology: “Analogous to this ‘Rule of thirds,’ (if I may be allowed so to call it) I have presumed to think that, in connecting or in breaking the various lines of a picture, it would likewise be a good rule to do it, in general, by a similar scheme of proportion.”

The camera made it a law

Photography manuals picked it up, film schools taught it, and eventually cameras and phones drew the grid onto the screen itself. Put the subject on a line, never in the middle. A tentative note about landscape painting became the first thing every beginner is told about the frame.

And the screen itself got a shape

The American studios adopted the Academy aperture in 1931 and the Academy codified it the next year, giving the near-square 1.37:1 frame. When television arrived in the 1950s, cinema went wide to compete, and the frame has been changing shape ever since. The Grand Budapest Hotel uses that history as a clock.

Six ways to arrange a frame

Centuries of painting and a century of cinema have trained the eye to read some arrangements as natural and others as deliberate, and how much of that is culture and how much is instinct is a different debate. Altering any of them is not a mistake, it is a different effect. The one thing worth holding onto is that where a film puts things changes what you feel about them, whether anyone meant it to or not.

Most films use several of these in a single scene. What marks a filmmaker is which one they reach for when it matters.

Why these films

Part Two starts with the most basic decision in filmmaking: where to put the camera, and what to leave out. Both films throw away the rule of thirds, one to show you a perfect world, the other to hide a private one.
