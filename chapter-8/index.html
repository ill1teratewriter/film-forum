Chapter 8 · Performance. Is great acting becoming someone else, or simply being there?

The two roads

Straight on, or a left at the Vista

Hollywood Boulevard runs east until it hits Sunset, and there is a theatre standing on the corner where they meet. Every chapter the room decides at that corner.

Two films about young men pulled into violence, and two opposite answers to the oldest argument in acting. In Joker, Joaquin Phoenix disappears completely: fifty-two pounds lost, a laugh built to hurt, and the Oscar for the most total transformation of his generation.

City of God was cast almost entirely with young people from Rio de Janeiro’s favelas, most of whom had never acted, trained in a workshop and then asked to improvise rather than recite. One performance is built over months. The other is closer to life.

Hollywood Blvd. Joker Not selected 2019 · 122 min · dir. Todd Phillips · written by Todd Phillips & Scott Silver · Warner Bros.

Gotham, 1981. A failed comedian with a condition that makes him laugh uncontrollably is ignored and beaten down by the city, until he stops trying to be ignored.

Download the Joker paper edition (PDF)

Why it is on the ballot. It is the purest example of the modern transformation performance: body, voice, laugh and walk all rebuilt from the ground up. Whether you think it is a masterpiece or too much, you will not see Joaquin Phoenix in it.

01

Before you press play

Optional trivia.

He lost 52 pounds

Phoenix dropped 52 lb (23 kg), eating roughly one sparse meal a day, and describes the weight loss as a way into the character rather than a stunt: “Losing weight affects how you feel. I’ll feel hungry. It’ll create that constant lack of satisfaction that’s so integral to the Joker’s personality.”

The laugh is built from real footage

He studied videos of people with pathological laughing and crying, the condition the press identified as pseudobulbar affect. The film never names it: Arthur simply carries a card explaining “a condition that causes sudden, frequent, uncontrollable laughter.” Phoenix plays the laugh as pain rather than symptom.

A comic-book film won the top prize at Venice

It took the Golden Lion in 2019, then two Oscars from eleven nominations — Phoenix for Best Actor and Hildur Guðnadóttir for Best Original Score — and became the first R-rated film to pass a billion dollars worldwide.

02

While you watch

Open each one only when you reach it.

The opening A smile made by hand Open

Arthur sits at a mirror and pulls his mouth into a smile with his fingers.

Why it matters: The film opens on a man performing. An actor playing a man who cannot stop acting is a hall of mirrors worth keeping in mind.

Every laughing fit Watch the eyes Open

When the laugh takes over, watch his eyes and hands rather than his mouth.

Why it matters: The face and the body are telling two different stories at once. That split is the whole performance.

The bathroom He starts to dance Open

After the worst night of his life, Arthur locks himself in a public bathroom and begins to move.

Why it matters: The dance replaced a scripted scene. Guðnadóttir had written the cello theme from the screenplay before shooting; Phillips played it on set and Phoenix improvised to it. She later said: “Phoenix just told me a couple of weeks ago that this was the moment where he was really able to find the character.”

The stairs Up, then down Open

Early on Arthur drags himself up a long outdoor staircase. Later he comes down it.

Why it matters: Same location, same actor, two completely different bodies. You can read the arc in the walk.

03

After the credits

Everything here gives the film away. Keep it for afterwards.

Open The music directed the performance Open

Guðnadóttir’s score existed before a frame was shot and was played to the actors on set, which is the reverse of the usual order. She described watching the rushes: “You could tell that the dance was choreographed to the music… he was basically experiencing the same movements that I had experienced when I was writing the music.”

Open The talk-show host is a mirror Open

Robert De Niro plays Murray Franklin, the host. In Scorsese’s The King of Comedy (1982), De Niro played Rupert Pupkin, the unstable fan stalking the host. The film hands him the other chair.

Open The staircase became a landmark Open

The steps on West 167th Street in Highbridge, the Bronx, turned into a tourist site almost overnight, with visitors recreating the dance, to the irritation of people who live there.

Sunset Blvd. City of God Not selected 2002 · 130 min · dir. Fernando Meirelles, co-directed by Kátia Lund · Portuguese · watch with subtitles on

Two boys grow up in the same Rio de Janeiro housing project across the 1960s and 70s. One wants to be a photographer. The other wants to run the place.

Download the City of God paper edition (PDF)

Why it is on the ballot. Where Joker is one actor transforming over months, this is a cast of first-timers who were never asked to become anyone. Most came from neighbourhoods like the one on screen, and the result is some of the most alive acting you will ever see from people who had never been in front of a camera.

01

Before you press play

Optional trivia.

The cast came from workshops, not agencies

Scouts toured Rio’s favelas with VHS cameras, and around a hundred young people were chosen for a workshop run by Kátia Lund and the acting coach Fátima Toledo. Accounts of its length vary from four or five months to a year. Only one professional actor, Matheus Nachtergaele, is in the main cast. The project that grew out of the workshop, Nós do Cinema, kept going after the film.

They never got a script

Meirelles: “The boys never got or read a script. In the process of rehearsing, we would start creating the scene, then reshaping the scene, then I would ask them to improvise.” The dialogue sounds like people talking because it mostly is.

It could not be shot where it is set

Meirelles: “We shot it in the City of God, but we had a terrible night. We were kidnapped by drug dealers and it traumatised part of the crew… we realised that we couldn’t shoot in the City of God, so we looked for other communities.” Most of the film was made in Cidade Alta and elsewhere.

02

While you watch

Open each one only when you reach it.

The opening The chicken Open

A knife is sharpened, a chicken escapes, and a whole street chases it until a young man with a camera is caught in the middle.

Why it matters: The first two minutes tell you this film runs on energy, not polish. Nobody moves like they have hit a mark. Part of the chase was a genuine accident that the camera simply followed — cinematographer César Charlone: “Let’s say that God is more creative than we are.”

The apartment One room, many years Open

The film tells the history of a single apartment from one position as its owners change, the walls darken and the place decays.

Why it matters: The actors come and go and the room stays. The film is as interested in the place as in any one performer.

The youngest kids Watch their faces Open

The children in the gangs are played by children.

Why it matters: They are not performing menace the way a trained actor would, which is exactly what makes them frightening. One scene involving two small boys is the hardest thing in the film to watch, and it is hard because nobody in it looks like they are acting.

The camera Rocket takes a picture Open

Keep track of when the narrator picks up his camera and what he points it at.

Why it matters: The one character who wants to watch rather than act is the one who survives to tell it. The still photograph that ends his arc was actually taken by Alexandre Rodrigues himself, after the production trained him on the camera.

03

After the credits

Everything here gives the film away. Keep it for afterwards.

Open The end credits show the real people Open

The film closes with television news footage of the real man one of the characters is based on, interviewed in jail. It is a jolt, and it tells you how close the cast stood to the story.

Open Four Oscar nominations, a year late Open

Released in Brazil in 2002, it was Brazil’s submission for Best Foreign Language Film and was not nominated. Its delayed American release made it eligible the following year, when it took four nominations — Director, Adapted Screenplay, Cinematography and Film Editing — and won none.

Open What happened to the cast is a mixed story Open

Alexandre Rodrigues and Leandro Firmino both kept working — Firmino won Best Actor at the Havana festival and has had a continuous Brazilian screen career; Rodrigues has worked steadily in film, television and theatre, with more recognition abroad than at home. Most of the wider cast did not get the same chances.

The lesson

Feel it for real

A Russian director described a system

Konstantin Stanislavski, co-founder of the Moscow Art Theatre, spent decades writing down how his actors worked: imagining the character’s circumstances, finding an objective in every scene, sometimes drawing on their own memories. An Actor Prepares reached English readers in 1936 — as a single volume he had never intended, since he meant it and Building a Character to be one book. He kept revising the system for the rest of his life.

An American teacher made it the Method

In New York, Lee Strasberg took one piece of that system, affective memory, and made it the centre of a technique. As artistic director of the Actors Studio from 1951 he shaped a generation of Studio actors, among them James Dean, Paul Newman and Al Pacino. In his words: “The two areas of discovery that were of primary importance in my work at the Actors Studio and in my private classes were improvisation and affective memory.”

The argument started immediately

Stella Adler spent five weeks studying with Stanislavski in Paris in 1934 and came back reporting that he had moved on: actors should build emotion from the given circumstances of the scene and from imagination, not by digging up their own grief. She and Strasberg taught opposing schools for the rest of their careers. The rule that grew out of Strasberg’s version — real acting means real feeling, and the proof is transformation — is still roughly how the Oscars vote.

Three ways to give a performance

One

Transform

The actor rebuilds body, voice and mind to become someone else.

Raging Bull · There Will Be Blood · Joker
Two

Be present

Non-professionals cast for who they are and allowed to behave rather than perform.

Bicycle Thieves · The Florida Project · City of God
Three

Be a star

The actor brings the same persona to every role and the film uses it.

Cary Grant · Tom Cruise · Keanu Reeves

None of the three is cheating. The question is which one the film needed.

One piece of vocabulary

Neorealism: the Italian postwar movement that shot in real streets with non-professional actors. Bicycle Thieves (1948) cast Lamberto Maggiorani, a factory worker, as its lead — and then he was laid off from the factory, never built a career, and ended up in bit parts. City of God is one of that movement’s great descendants, and it raises the same question about what happens to people after the film.

Why these films

Chapter 7 was the character on the page. This chapter is the person who has to become it on screen, and the old argument about how. Joker is the Method at its most extreme. City of God asks whether you need acting at all.
